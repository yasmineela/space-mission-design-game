**Status: NO human playtesting has been done yet.** Everything below is preparation. Every results table in this kit is intentionally empty. Do not fill anything in from assumption.

### Protecting the original save
The developer's own game lives at `http://localhost:5173/` (the dev server). **Never use that address for playtests.**
Use the production build on a different port instead, so its saved data is completely separate:

```bash
cd "<path to the Space Buddy project>"
npm run build
npm run preview -- --host --port 4173
```

- Laptop on the same machine: open `http://localhost:4173/`
- Other devices on the same Wi-Fi: open `http://<this-computer's-IP>:4173/` (the terminal prints it as "Network").
- Stop the server with `Ctrl+C` when finished.

### Clean starting state for every participant
Pick one method and use it every time:
1. **Best:** open a **new private / incognito window** at the test address for each child, and close it afterwards (storage is discarded).
2. Or use a **different browser profile** per child.
3. Or, between children, clear the site data for the test address only (browser settings → site data → `localhost:4173` / the IP address).

Check before each session: the welcome screen shows **only** the "START YOUR MISSION" button (no "Restore my previous game"). If "Restore my previous game" appears, the window is not clean – do not use it.

Do not give children the in-game "Start a new mission / reset" option as a way to start over; it is meant for players, not for session set-up.

## 3. Session plan (20–30 minutes per participant)

**Rules for the facilitator:** read the script text exactly; do not explain the game; do not point at things; if the child asks "what do I do?", answer "What do you think you should do?" and write the question down. Step in only if the child is upset, or stuck for **more than 2 minutes** (then note the time, give the smallest hint, and record it).

### Stage A – First impressions (3 min)
Say: *"This is a game. Have a look around and play with it however you like. I'll watch. You can tell me what you're thinking out loud if you want."*
Do not explain the game.
Observe and note: where they look/click first; whether they find the goal ("build a spaceship and fly to Mars") on their own; whether they find the **next-step box**; where they hesitate; any questions they ask.
Ask at the end of the stage (neutral): *"What do you think this game is about?"*

### Stage B – Gameplay exploration (7 min)
Say: *"Now try these things in any order: say hello to your spaceship; try the Science Academy; answer a question; see if you can get a reward; and have a look at what you could build."*
Observe: do they know how to interact with the companion; can they find and start an Academy question; what do they do after a wrong answer (retry, hint, give up?); do they notice and understand the reward (+credits); can they find the Workshop and understand parts and prices; do they hit the 'not enough credits' moment, and what do they do.
Record time to first completed Academy question, and time to first purchase.

### Stage C – Mission experience (7 min)
Say: *"Let's go on a mission. Try to take your spaceship as far as you can."* (From a clean start a child will not reach the journey in 20–30 minutes. Decide in advance, and record it for every child, whether Stage C uses the optional **launched demo save** (see §2b) or just whatever Mission Control / training screens the child can reach.)
Observe: Are the instructions clear? Do they understand what each challenge is asking? Do they understand the consequence of their choice (fuel, energy, etc.)? Is the explanation after a choice understandable? Does a wrong choice help them learn?
Ask after one challenge: *"Why do you think that happened?"*

### Stage D – Companion and engagement (5 min)
Say: *"Spend a few minutes with your spaceship friend."*
Observe: do they tap, talk to, feed or play with it; do they notice mood changes (face, coloured ring, speech); do they know what it needs; any emotional reactions (smiling, laughing, "aww", worry).
Ask: *"How do you think your spaceship is feeling? How can you tell?"*

### Stage E – Final feedback (5 min)
Ask exactly these, one at a time, and write the child's own words:
1. What did you enjoy most?
2. What was confusing?
3. Was anything frustrating?
4. Did you learn something new? (If yes: *"What was it?"*)
5. Which feature would you improve?
6. Would you play Space Buddy again? Why / why not?
7. What would make you want to play again?

Do not suggest answers. Do not react to answers as good or bad. Say "Thank you, that really helps."

## 4. Observer guidelines

- **Be silent.** Wait at least 30 seconds before helping. Count slowly.
- **Record behaviour, not opinions.** Write what the child *did* ("tapped the ship 4 times, then opened Academy") plus short quotes.
- **Log hesitation.** Note the screen and the time whenever the child pauses > 5 seconds, says "what?", sighs, or clicks the wrong place.
- **Log repeated mistakes** (same error twice) and misunderstandings (child states something false about the game or the science).
- **Log attention:** what they return to, laugh at, show a friend, or ask to do again.
- **Time things:** first interaction; first completed question; first purchase; first challenge.
- **Mark every intervention** (what you said, when).
- **Separate individual taste from problems.** One child disliking something is a *preference*. A *problem* is something several children trip over.
- **Never pressure** a child to be positive. "I didn't like it" is useful data. Let them stop at any time without explanation.
- Do not compare children, or tell them how others did.

## 5. Recruitment: how many and who

- Aim for **5–8 children** across the range: e.g. two aged 9–10, two aged 11–12, two aged 13, plus mix of confident and hesitant gamers, and boys and girls where possible.
- Aim for at least **two on a phone** and **two on a laptop/tablet**. This matters for the rescue game and button size.
- Record age **band** only (9–10, 11–12, 13), device, and prior gaming experience (none / some / lots). Nothing else.

## 6. Consent and privacy (adapt to your local rules)

- Get **written permission from a parent or guardian** before the session, and ask the child if they are happy to take part ("assent"). Tell both that they can stop at any time.
- **Do not collect** names, school, address, photos or voice recordings unless the guardian specifically agrees to recording in writing, and say how it will be stored and deleted.
- Use participant codes **P01, P02, …** on all notes. Keep any list linking codes to people (if one exists at all) separate from the notes, and destroy it when no longer needed.
- The game stores data only in the browser on the device. Tell families that the game does not collect personal information, and that you will clear the browser data after each session.
- Remind the child to use a nickname if they are asked for a name in the game ("never your real name").
- Store scorecards securely; delete or anonymise per your organisation's policy.

Suggested guardian wording: *"We are testing a children's space-science game. Your child will play for about 25 minutes while an adult watches and takes notes. Participation is voluntary and your child can stop at any time. We will record age band, device and what your child does in the game, not their name. [Add recording/photo details here, if any.] Do you agree? Yes / No – Signature / date."*

## 7. Scorecard

Printable version: `scorecard.html`. Scale for every category:
**1** = very difficult / poor · **2** = difficult / below expectations · **3** = acceptable · **4** = good · **5** = excellent.

Categories: Understanding the objective · Navigation and usability · Gameplay enjoyment · Educational value · Companion engagement · Mission clarity · Visual appeal · Overall experience.

The **observer** rates each category from what they saw (not from the child's mood), and notes the child's own rating if they give one. Keep the two separate.

---

## 8. Analysis method (use only after real sessions)

1. Enter each participant's scores and notes in the results table (§9).
2. Group every observation into: **Positive experiences · Confusing interactions · Technical problems · Educational difficulties · Features enjoyed · Suggestions**.
3. Count **how many participants** met each issue (e.g. "4 of 6"). An issue seen by **1** child is recorded as "single observation" – investigate, but do not call it a defect.
4. Assign a priority:
   - **Critical** – prevents gameplay or loses progress.
   - **High** – repeatedly confuses or frustrates players (rule of thumb: ≥ 3 participants or 40%+ of those who reached it).
   - **Medium** – affects enjoyment or usability.
   - **Low** – optional visual / preference.
5. For each recommendation fill in the template in §10. **Do not change** mechanics, scientific content, scoring or rewards unless the observations give a clear reason, and agree changes before implementing.
6. Compute average category scores only from real scorecards, and always show the number of participants next to them.

## 12. Known limitations of this preparation
- Nothing in this kit has been used with a child yet. The question list and timings are the developer's plan, not findings.
- A 25-minute session cannot reach the end of the Earth-to-Mars mission from a clean start. Stage C may only reach the mission screens the child has unlocked; decide in advance whether to allow a pre-built mid-mission save for Stage C and record which was used.
- With 5–8 children, results show patterns, not statistics.
